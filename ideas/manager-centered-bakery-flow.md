# Idea Landscape — Manager-Centered Bakery Flow

State: `expanding`  
Origin: Matthew  
Captured: 2026-07-24  
Exploration partners: Matthew + Mike

## The idea in Matthew's words

The bakery has two connected problems: insufficient ordering can cause shortages, while irregular quantities of pastries are wasted each day. The original concept gave the baker, manager, and food runner separate devices so they could send requests about low display stock, low ingredients, or making fewer pastries. Matthew noticed that this could create a digital bottleneck and interrupt a fast-paced workflow.

A later direction keeps the already-working verbal communication between food runner and baker while giving the manager a digital planning view. The manager can review pastry and frozen-stock information, compare similar sales days, count full and partial freezer boxes, consider freezer capacity, and decide what supplies may need to be ordered. Physical checks still reconcile what the system estimates with what is actually present.

## Why it feels interesting

Matthew is not merely designing screens. He is questioning whether adding software to every role actually improves the work. The central possibility is a system that supports better decisions without replacing communication that is already fast.

## Boldest version

If it worked perfectly, the bakery would have a calm, trustworthy operating picture even during busy periods. The manager would understand what is likely to run out, what is accumulating, what the freezer can hold, and why the system made each recommendation. Bakers and food runners could keep moving quickly, while important exceptions still become useful planning evidence without forcing constant device use.

## Full world / journey

- Before: Historical sales, the latest physical freezer count, incoming stock, supplier rules, and known unusual events establish the planning context.
- During: The food runner and baker continue fast verbal coordination. When the display is low, they may either bring already-finished pastries from the back or bake another batch, depending on available stock and time. Important exceptions such as sellouts, extra batches, or unusual damage could be captured later or through a very low-friction method.
- After: The manager compares expected and actual outcomes, performs a physical reconciliation, and adjusts future ordering or production assumptions.
- A perfect day with this idea: The manager makes an informed plan, frontline staff work without digital interruption, the display stays adequately stocked, and leftovers remain within an understood target rather than varying without explanation.

## People and roles it could touch

- Manager: Personally performs the physical freezer count shortly before deciding whether to place a supplier order. Matthew reports that the manager usually reviews POS reports and uses personal judgment when preparing the order. This confirms a judgment-led process rather than a fixed automatic reorder rule. It is still unknown whether supplier cutoffs/delivery days are fixed, whether stock levels trigger the timing, or whether both apply; do not infer that detail from the manager's use of POS reports. The workflow can show the relevant supplier timing when known; confirm full/partial box counts; recalculate products needing restock; review suggested boxes, supplier constraints, and freezer capacity; then approve the internal draft. Before count confirmation, the restock KPI should visibly say `Waiting for freezer count` or `Using an older count` rather than presenting stale recommendations as current.
- Baker/head baker: Receives a clear production target while retaining operational judgment about timing, trays, and reserves.
- Food runner/frontline: Restocks the display and uses the existing fast verbal path for immediate shortages or sellouts.
- Owner: Sees longer-term patterns in stockouts, waste, purchasing, and process reliability.
- Customer: Encounters fewer unavailable products while freshness remains protected.
- Supplier/other ecosystem roles: Supplier schedules, case sizes, minimums, and lead times constrain the manager's decision; no order is sent automatically.
- Other possible users: A shift lead could use a read-only or handoff view when the manager is unavailable.

## Expanding branches

### Branch A — Manager supplier-restock planner

Matthew has chosen to resume exploring this branch under the clearer working name **Next Order List**. He specifically wants to revisit the already-built top-to-bottom KPI flow in the current prototype rather than restart discovery or redesign the workflow from scratch. His chosen interaction structure is scalable: the Overview shows a compact KPI card with the number/urgency of products needing reorder review; the entire KPI card is clickable and transitions directly to the inner Next Order List page. The Overview card must not attempt to list every affected product because the list may be long, and it should not require a separate `Expand` step. The inner page contains all affected products, evidence, suggested and manager-edited quantities, planning consequences, and internal approval.

Matthew added two adjacent workspace ideas. First, the workspace should use `Current` and **History** tabs. History contains only finalized internal Next Order Lists as read-only snapshots; unfinished drafts must not appear there. Second, the current workspace should support both **List** and **Chart** views: List remains the exact action surface for names and quantities, while Chart can compare shortage magnitude. A compact reorder widget can show a short list, use an internal scrollbar when long, and expand into a wide side/full-screen view.

Matthew also wants unfinished drafts visible from the main homepage. The clean interpretation is one shared draft exposed in two places: the homepage provides a `Continue draft` entry point, while the `Current` workspace is where that same draft is edited and finalized. Do not create duplicate draft records or a separate Drafts tab unless the workflow later requires multiple simultaneous drafts. One possible simplification is for the existing Next Order List KPI card to change state to `Draft in progress` rather than showing a redundant second card. Whether the bakery needs one active draft or multiple concurrent drafts remains open.

This is an exploration/review decision, not yet approval to implement changes. The Next Order List is the manager-facing workflow or artifact; **Products at Risk Before the Next Ordering Opportunity** is the top KPI/entry signal inside that workflow rather than the workflow itself.

During engineering convergence, Matthew clarified that the fixed seven-day coverage value is only a placeholder. The real horizon should be calculated from the count/planning date-time to the estimated shipment-arrival date-time, including every weekday and weekend in between. Demand should still vary by the appropriate comparable-day evidence rather than treating weekdays and weekends as identical. When several pastries consume the same frozen-dough SKU, their expected dough usage must be aggregated before comparison with the physical dough count. Matthew further clarified that delivery does not occur on one consistent weekday and can vary with the order/customer-demand cycle, so each Next Order List draft needs its own estimated arrival date rather than a hard-coded supplier weekday. He confirmed that this entered date is the expected arrival date of the boxes selected in that same draft. Current stock can therefore be checked against expected usage from count/planning time until arrival. However, boxes arriving at the endpoint cannot prevent a pre-arrival shortage, so the `Short before delivery` signal must remain separate from the quantity needed after arrival.

For the arriving quantity, Matthew wants a reasonable middle ground: enough dough to support expected customer demand with some protection against selling out, but not so much that slow-selling inventory ties up cash or crowds the freezer. Since usable frozen dough is not immediately wasted, this is an excess-inventory/working-capital/capacity risk rather than a true sunk cost. A promising manager-facing model is `This shipment arrives` plus `Plan stock through`, allowing variable dates. Matthew selected the current production-sheet quantity for each included date as the V1 expected-demand baseline; comparable-day POS sales remain supporting evidence and a later baseline-improvement path rather than silently replacing the sheet with a mean or median. For V1, the entered shipment date is the first date on which the selected boxes are treated as available; earlier dates are pre-arrival and that date onward is post-arrival, with time-of-day complexity deferred. Expected post-arrival production-sheet usage across that window, projected stock remaining at arrival, whole-box rounding, supplier minimums, and practical freezer capacity can then support a transparent suggested quantity. The manager retains the final editable quantity and no hidden percentage buffer is added. Production and waste recommendation formulas remain separate later discussions; current convergence is focused on Next Order List.

The supplier-restock decision asks: **Will current frozen stock cover expected customer demand until the manager's next opportunity to order, without buying products that are unlikely to sell?** Matthew clarified that the manager does not try to reach a fixed stock target or simply make a shipment last until the next order. The problem is deciding, product by product, whether expected sales before the next ordering opportunity will use more than the current stock. Matthew reports that the manager currently combines sales, production information, and personal experience through individual judgment. He identifies two concrete problems: the process requires too much mental calculation, and the resulting order can still create too much or too little stock. Too little frozen stock means the bakery cannot make the affected pastry; if customer demand exists, this creates potential lost sales and gross margin. Too much usable frozen stock is mainly a freezer-space problem rather than waste, sunk cost, or confirmed lost profit; Matthew reports no meaningful expiration or quality limit from remaining frozen too long. The product opportunity is therefore to organize scattered evidence, perform repeated comparisons and calculations, prepare a transparent draft, and help the manager balance product-availability risk against projected freezer crowding while retaining final judgment and overrides. A possible primary KPI card is **Products at Risk Before the Next Ordering Opportunity**, with one dominant count and a `Review supplier restock` drill-down. The top-down worksheet can show each product's physical full/partial box count, expected sales/usage over the relevant period, projected shortfall or surplus, suggested boxes, and plain-language evidence. The manager edits and approves an internal draft quantity; no external order is sent automatically. The opposite-risk guardrail is **Projected Freezer Capacity After Order**. The approved production sheet and comparable sales can feed expected dough usage into this calculation, but the supplier-restock worksheet remains a separate manager decision surface so changing an order does not silently change production.

### Branch B — Manager production planner

Comparable-day product sales, leftovers, sellouts, extra batches, and unusual events help the manager prepare tomorrow's pastry quantities. A final read-only or printed list supports bakers without requiring them to interpret the forecast.

Matthew reports that pastry quantities are currently set by day on a production sheet and rarely change. He sees this fixed sheet as a useful baseline rather than a reason to ignore changing demand: weather, events, and predicted customer traffic could support explainable `+ / −` adjustments. His primary problem and intended outcome for this branch is reducing the cost of finished-pastry leftovers, while still watching for sold-outs so cost reduction does not create larger lost-sales problems.

Matthew has now clarified that the **Manager Production Planner should mainly prepare tomorrow's production**. Comparable weekdays can still provide evidence for tomorrow's recommendation—for example, prior Wednesdays informing the upcoming Wednesday—without becoming a separate headline KPI. Matthew agreed to keep this evidence inside Tomorrow's Production Planner for now and is interested in showing it visually as a product-level comparable-days chart. A product enters `Needs review` when the suggested quantity differs from the current production-sheet quantity or when an unusual condition/change makes the normal plan questionable. Matthew wants two distinct review labels: **`Unusual context`** for promotions, events, weather, unexpected demand spikes, sellouts, or other non-normal operating conditions; and **`Large quantity change`** when the suggested increase or decrease is unusually large. A product may carry both labels. A normal nonzero suggested change can use a simpler **`Quantity change`** label. Matthew identifies weak evidence as missing leftover information, only one comparable day, a promotion that makes a prior day non-normal, or a newly introduced pastry with little/no history. These cases should stay visible under a separate **`Limited evidence`** warning and require manager judgment rather than being presented as confident recommendations.

Matthew chose an explicit per-product review interaction to make decisions easy and unambiguous. Every flagged product offers **`Use suggestion`**, **`Keep current`**, and **`Set custom quantity`**. Merely opening a row or chart does not count as review. Choosing one action marks that product reviewed. After all required products have a manager decision, one **`Finalize tomorrow's production plan`** button finalizes the entire internal sheet at once rather than finalizing products individually. This remains an internal approval; no live production record is changed or sent automatically. After finalization, Matthew wants **both** a read-only on-screen baker sheet and a printable baker sheet. Both surfaces must render the same finalized plan so quantities cannot diverge. The baker-facing versions should be operationally simple and should not require bakers to interpret manager KPI logic or comparable-day charts.

For **ready-to-bake pastries only**, Matthew wants the baker sheet to split each finalized quantity into an **opening batch** and a **reserve batch**. The opening batch takes significant preparation time and needs to be baked, ready, and put out before **9:00 AM**. Matthew clarified that the ready-to-bake reserve is **thawed or otherwise prepared, but not yet baked**; it is prepared capacity for later replenishment rather than finished production. The sheet should therefore make the opening deadline visually dominant and distinguish `Opening batch — bake for 9:00 AM`, `Reserve — prepare, do not bake yet`, and `Total prepared/planned` for eligible pastries. Other pastry/product types should show a simple finalized quantity unless their real workflow later establishes a different split; do not force the ready-to-bake pattern onto cakes or unrelated products. The system must not imply that all reserve units were baked or count unbaked reserve as finished-pastry leftovers. What happens to a ready-to-bake reserve that remains unbaked at the end of the day still needs confirmation because it affects waste and next-day planning.

A promising chart form is one bar per recent comparable weekday, showing sold units plus leftovers against the current production-sheet baseline, with explicit sellout markers and a distinct proposed-tomorrow quantity. The chart is an explanatory evidence component, not the KPI itself. To draw it honestly, each displayed day needs the date/weekday, produced quantity, sold quantity, leftover quantity when available, sellout status, and any unusual context such as a promotion. Missing values must be shown as missing rather than silently treated as zero. The chart should live in the selected product's evidence panel, retain exact values and plain-language explanation, and visibly label excluded or unusual days rather than imply false certainty.

A separate next-comparable-weekday feature remains an open secondary branch: it would earn a distinct KPI only if it detects a repeated weekday-specific pattern that suggests the standard production-sheet baseline itself should change. Possible forms include `Products repeatedly overproduced on comparable weekdays`, `Repeated comparable-day waste cost`, or `Baseline quantities needing review`. This would be a slower learning/baseline-maintenance surface rather than an urgent daily production-planning action. Do not promote it or crowd the homepage with it unless a repeated weekday-specific operational problem is confirmed.

Matthew reports that same-day replenishment is usually judged from both **weekday/weekend** and **time of day**. On weekdays, items become harder to sell after about 3:00 p.m. than they do on weekends. The food runner currently uses judgment to decide whether more items should be brought out or made. This is a reported operating rule, not yet a fixed formula.

Matthew's goal is to reduce waste by translating leftovers into simple financial numbers the manager can understand. In Matthew's words, the dashboard could show the “opportunity cost of sales earned.” Matthew distinguishes **retail value** as the customer selling price associated with the leftover items and **waste cost** as the bakery's unrecovered supplier cost for those items. These can be shown side by side because they answer different questions. They should not be added into one total-loss figure: the selling price is designed to recover the supplier cost plus margin, so adding supplier cost again would count that cost twice. Leftovers also do not prove that customers would have bought every item, so **unsold retail value** or **retail value of leftovers** is more honest than confirmed “sales lost.”

Matthew wants the Waste Review to follow the same scalable interaction pattern as the other manager KPIs: the **entire homepage KPI card is clickable** and opens a focused inner page. His initial headline idea was a money amount showing how much was wasted, but he then correctly challenged whether that number identifies a specific manager problem. Supplier cost in finished-pastry leftovers describes financial impact but does not by itself reveal what behaved abnormally, why, or what action to take. Matthew's emerging problem-oriented direction is therefore an **irregular comparable-day pattern**: flag pastries whose finished leftovers behave unusually versus reliable recent comparable weekdays, while keeping supplier waste cost as secondary impact context. A possible card is `2 pastries had unusually high leftovers compared with recent Wednesdays`, supported by `$X supplier cost affected`. This remains exploratory because “unusual” needs a trustworthy baseline and several weeks of comparable-day data; before enough history exists, the feature should say `Building comparable-day baseline` rather than invent an anomaly.

A product-level comparable-days chart is beneficial only if it helps the manager validate the exception—for example, by showing the current day against a normal-range band, prior comparable-day points/bars, sold versus finished leftovers, sellout markers, and promotions/events. A generic sales chart is not enough because low sales may reflect low production or a sellout. One abnormal day should trigger investigation, not an automatic production cut. The inner page should help distinguish a one-off contextual exception from a repeated production-baseline problem. The Waste Review should diagnose financial impact and repeated leftover patterns without duplicating the editable Tomorrow's Production decision surface; any production change should be reviewed/finalized in that separate workspace. Matthew confirmed that this problem definition and interaction match what he wants, so the Waste Pattern Review concept is now selected while real thresholds, baseline requirements, and data integration remain open for implementation validation.

Matthew thinks the most helpful manager outcome is to bake less on the corresponding weekday the following week. One possible surface would therefore lead with an explainable next-comparable-day production adjustment, while showing leftover units and waste cost as evidence. In this structure, **“bake fewer next Tuesday” is a recommendation rather than a KPI**. A primary waste KPI could be waste cost or waste rate across comparable weekdays; unsold retail value could be supporting context; sales revenue and sellout frequency could be guardrails that reveal whether waste reduction is harming genuine sales. The decision loop becomes: KPI detects waste → recommendation proposes a production change → the following comparable weekday's KPI shows whether the change helped. The view could also retain other contextual branches—unusual events, weather, promotions, and weekday/time patterns—so reducing waste does not accidentally create shortages.

### Branch C — Exception-only learning loop

Normal verbal work remains unchanged. Only unusual events—sellout, extra batch, major damage, unexpected leftovers, delivery discrepancy—are captured, either by the manager after the rush or through a very low-friction shared method.

Matthew wants to explore rush-time exceptions next. He reports that this type of catch-up mainly applies to frozen, ready-to-bake pastries: a baker can take the pastries from the freezer, thaw them for roughly 5–10 minutes, and then bake them. The food runner's job is to warn the baker before the display runs out, and Matthew reports that actual runouts are rare because the extra batch is normally started early enough. This means the advance verbal replenishment loop is a working normal process, not itself an exception or obvious software problem, and it should not be replaced by routine digital approval or alerts.

A true rush exception would be a failure or limit of that normal loop—for example, late or missed notice, an unusually fast demand spike, insufficient frozen stock, unavailable oven/tray capacity, or an extra batch that still does not arrive before the display empties. Matthew reports that the food runner mainly decides when to warn the baker through personal experience and judgment rather than a fixed threshold. He does not currently know whether this causes inconsistency or training difficulty across different/newer food runners. Therefore that concern remains explicitly unknown rather than a confirmed problem or feature requirement. Current evidence only supports that actual runouts are rare and the normal advance-warning process usually works.

This creates a possible product classification rather than one universal rush rule: ready-to-bake items can recover relatively quickly, while other pastries may have different preparation constraints. One possibility is that replenishment flexibility affects how conservatively the manager sets the initial production quantity; another is that true extra-batch failures reveal where the normal process underestimates demand. These remain exploratory possibilities, not selected features or recommendations.

### Branch D — Shared physical-space model

The freezer is shared by frozen-dough boxes, packaged-dessert boxes, cake-batter containers, macaron boxes, smoothie mixes, and fillings. Matthew reports that these objects have meaningfully different dimensions and assigned freezer sections. Section boundaries are strict, although each section has some legroom within its boundary. The section limits are not currently established and would need a one-time physical setup: zone name and allowed contents, zone-specific counting unit, normal/practical capacity, and hard maximum. The manager is authorized to change these limits later without owner approval. Because changes affect capacity warnings and restock recommendations, the app could show old versus new values, require explicit confirmation, recalculate any current draft, and retain a simple change history. The saved limits support routine counts without asking the manager to re-measure dimensions. The space between practical and hard capacity is temporary legroom, not interchangeable capacity from another category's section. The manager's count establishes current occupancy, and a draft supplier order projects incoming dough into the relevant assigned section. A manager-facing guardrail could count **Zones needing capacity review after draft order** and show `comfortable` below practical capacity, `limited` when using legroom, and `blocked` above the hard maximum. A visual shelf/zone view can retain manager confirmation while avoiding exact dimension entry during every count.

### Branch E — Waste and shrinkage model

Small, stable damage can be represented as a transparent shrinkage allowance. Large or unusual damage becomes an exception. Finished-pastry leftovers remain separate from damaged frozen dough because they explain different problems.

### Branch F — Calm shared awareness

Instead of three full applications, a read-only shared display, printed sheet, kiosk, voice draft, or QR exception action could provide visibility without requiring every worker to manage an inbox.

Matthew wants to explore a different KPI that helps the manager notice a problem repeating across several days, but he has not yet identified a specific repeated problem from direct observation. Keep this branch broad rather than choosing a metric first. Each possibility requires its own evidence, consequence, and manager action before it earns a KPI. The branch may become a weekly exception review, a pattern detector, or something else entirely; no interface or metric has been selected.

### Branch H — Phone-native capture: photos and voice

Captured 2026-08-07. Matthew wants to explore giving the iPhone itself a real job in the workflow, starting from one spark: **letting the phone take pictures of leftovers so the manager (or whoever is looking) can see leftovers quickly**, rather than needing to physically inspect or write them down. Matthew also wants to explore voice commands more broadly on the phone, not just for leftovers.

This connects to two things already sitting in the landscape: the data-entry principle from `references/workplace-workflow-discovery-2026-07-20.md` — *"Photo and voice input may prepare draft records, but a human should confirm uncertain counts or interpreted changes"* — and Branch E (Waste and shrinkage model) and the Waste Pattern Review concept, both of which depend on knowing what leftovers actually look like at the end of a day. This branch is still fully open; nothing below is chosen or scoped.

This is exploration only — not yet connected to the current Next Order List BUILD lane, and not a commitment to build anything.

**Photo-of-leftovers spark, expanded:** captured at end of day, by anyone with a phone (food runner, manager, or otherwise — not role-restricted). The photo is intended to auto-count the leftovers, with the human accepting or editing the resulting number rather than trusting it blindly — matching the existing data-entry principle that photo/voice input prepares a draft, not a final record.

**Voice, narrowed to one branch Matthew wants to pursue:** the manager speaking the physical freezer count out loud while counting (e.g., "five boxes and one piece") instead of typing it, motivated by the real physical constraint of counting inside a freezer, possibly with gloves on and hands occupied. Other voice possibilities were raised and left open rather than chosen: hands-free versions of the existing `Low`/`Sold Out`/`Extra Batch` one-tap actions, a spoken context/unusual-event note, a question-answering mode ("how much croissant dough do we have left"), and narrating alongside the leftover photo as a cross-check on the auto-count.

**Why this specific branch (voice-driven freezer counting) is strong portfolio/interview material:**

- It's a real speech-to-structured-data problem: turning spoken phrasing ("five boxes and one piece," "5 and 1," etc.) into the exact `{ fullBoxes, partialPieces }` shape the existing calculation engine expects — a genuine parsing/validation problem, not just "add a mic button."
- It forces a defensible architecture decision: on-device speech recognition (works with poor freezer wifi, keeps business data local, no per-call cost) versus a cloud speech API (potentially more accurate, but costs money and needs connectivity) — a concrete tradeoff tied to the actual physical environment (a walk-in freezer).
- It's a natural home for the human-in-the-loop pattern already established in this project: never silently trust an ambiguous input — show "I heard 5 boxes, 1 piece — confirm?" and let the human correct it, which is a product-judgment story, not just a coding one.
- It plugs into something already built rather than standing alone: the number it produces feeds directly into the pre-arrival/post-arrival formulas and the server-side draft, so it demonstrates integrating a new capability into existing architecture.
- It's cleanly testable: feed a list of transcribed strings into the parser and assert the right numbers come out every time — deterministic tests behind a fuzzy input, consistent with this project's test-first culture.

**Matthew's stated intent (2026-08-07):** he wants this branch included when implementation happens, not just left as a concept. This is recorded as intent, not yet a convergence decision — per the idea-lab rules, promoting it into the actual BUILD scope still requires an explicit later convergence discussion once the branch is more fully explored, rather than being folded in automatically.

**Leftover photo and freezer count are separate moments**, not one combined walkthrough. Matthew likes that daily leftover photos would build a visual history feeding the Waste Pattern Review branch's comparable-day evidence, not just numbers.

**Why breadth of features matters here, clarified:** Matthew asked whether having many features demonstrates skillset for an interview. The refined answer: feature *count* isn't the mechanism — each feature touching a genuinely different engineering skill (speech parsing, image-based counting, an API/database layer, a calculation engine with real edge cases), built with real depth rather than shallow demos, is what reads as strong. This shapes how future branches under this idea might eventually be selected, without narrowing anything yet.

**Multi-user question, opened and resolved for this idea (not yet a BUILD decision):** Matthew does not want to replace the food runner's verbal report with anything more complex — no digitized reporting logic for frontline roles. Instead, non-manager roles (starting with the food runner) get a deliberately stripped-down extension of the manager tool: **camera/photo capture and voice commands only** — not the full manager workspace (no Next Order List, no dashboards, no editing). The full manager app remains manager-only. This keeps the earlier project history's caution about not digitizing the already-fast verbal loop mostly intact — the food runner's only new job is pointing a camera, not learning a reporting interface.

**Manager review screen, first shape:** after a leftover photo is taken, the manager sees a list of inventory — pastry name alongside the expected (auto-counted) leftover quantity — which is where accept/edit would happen per the earlier-described human-in-the-loop pattern.

**Voice interaction model:** push-to-talk, not always-listening or wake-word activated. A button press starts listening, a button press stops it — the manager controls exactly when the phone is capturing speech.

## KPI and indicator landscape — keep branches open

Not every useful number needs to become a headline KPI. Matthew wants the first manager dashboard to prioritize **what is urgent now, followed by what is next most urgent**. This suggests an exception-first action queue rather than a grid where every KPI has equal visual weight. A possible manager homepage uses a compact header with store/date and data freshness, followed by three stacked groups: `Act now`, `Review today`, and `Plan next`. Each queue item combines the relevant KPI/status with the affected product or freezer zone, why it matters now, deadline/time horizon, confidence/freshness, and one next authorized action. Selecting an item opens the appropriate deeper workflow: waste review, production plan, freezer count, or supplier-restock worksheet. Urgency should depend on timing and operational consequence, not simply whichever metric has the largest number.

Possible waste-management measures include **Supplier Waste Cost** as a primary financial outcome, **Waste Rate by Product** for fair comparison across different production volumes, **Products Repeatedly Above Normal Waste** for recurring exceptions, and **Sellouts/Product Availability** as the opposite-risk guardrail. Possible supplier-restock measures include **Products at Shortage Risk** for the current decision, **Pastries Unavailable Because Frozen Stock Ran Out** as an actual outcome, **Freezer Zones Needing Capacity Review** as the space guardrail, and a later **Recommendation Accuracy** measure comparing expected usage with observed results. Supporting evidence such as unsold retail value, days of supply, suggested boxes, count freshness, and draft order cost can remain useful without occupying headline KPI cards.
